How the page unfolds: t begins with recorded observations, then explores conservation risk, important-use areas, seasonal patterns, interactions between protected wildlife and Commonwealth fisheries, and finally how important habitat overlaps Commonwealth marine parks.

Recorded wildlife clusters around the coast.

These charts begin with individual observations, then zoom out to show where downloaded evidence accumulates and how unevenly it is distributed between species.

Individual occurrence records show where wildlife has been reported.

Each dot is one Atlas of Living Australia observation.

These records show where wildlife has been observed, not how many animals live there.

Across the five focal species, there are 226,389 downloaded occurrence records. Green turtle accounts for 125,760, while grey nurse shark has only 108.

Uneven coverage can reflect survey effort, accessibility and data availability, as well as animal presence.

Observation evidence clusters in several coastal hotspots.

Circle size shows records. Colour shows how many focal species are represented.

The downloaded evidence is highly uneven between species.

Species totals show dataset coverage, not abundance.

Risk and pressure tell different stories.

Legal status describes assessed extinction risk, while documented threats describe the kinds of human or environmental pressures linked to each species in the source material.

Legal status and documented threat coverage do not line up neatly.

Each coloured line connects a species' official EPBC status on the left with how many of the five documented threat categories are linked to it on the right.

Grey nurse shark refers to the east coast population.

The eastern grey nurse shark is Critically Endangered and is linked to three of five documented threat categories.

The Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin is not nationally EPBC-listed as threatened here, yet it is linked to five of five documented threat categories.

More documented threat links do not automatically mean greater severity.

How to read: hover a node or line for details, or use the Focus species menu to isolate one species.

Several documented threat types are shared across species.

Fishing / bycatch, entanglement / debris and climate / heat each connect to several focal species. The network shows which documented threats recur across species, not how severe they are.

Each line connects one focal species on the left to one documented threat type on the right. Larger threat nodes mean that more focal species share that documented threat.

How to read: hover a node or line for details, and use the Focus species menu to highlight one species while dimming the rest.

Node size shows how many species share a documented threat type. It does not show severity.

Some waters matter more for different behaviours.

Biologically Important Areas identify mapped places used for behaviours such as migration, reproduction, foraging and resting. They are not complete species ranges.

Mapped important-use areas vary by species and behaviour.

Choose a species and behaviour. Colour explains why each mapped area matters. Some species-behaviour combinations are not available in the source data, so an empty map means no BIA is mapped for that selection.

Humpback whale BIAs are especially large for migration and reproduction, while Australian sea lion BIAs are concentrated around foraging.

Mapped area helps compare footprint, but it is not a measure of ecological importance or population size.

The map is best used to compare where particular behaviours are documented, rather than to infer a species' full range.

If a species-behaviour selection shows no polygon, it means that combination is not available in the source BIA data, not that the species is absent from those waters.

Mapped important area varies strongly across behaviours.

The heatmap compares mapped BIA area across species and behaviours. Cells marked N/A indicate that no mapped BIA value is available for that species-behaviour combination.

Area shows mapped footprint, not ecological importance.

Marine wildlife follows a seasonal rhythm.

Monthly patterns reveal timing in the downloaded records while preserving the important caveat that observations are not population counts.

The annual cycle highlights when records peak.

The polar view keeps December beside January so the annual cycle remains continuous.

Peak months differ: humpback whale in August, bottlenose dolphin in July, green turtle in May, and grey nurse shark and Australian sea lion in April.

These peaks may reflect animal movement, survey effort or reporting practices.

These are recording peaks, not population peaks.

Monthly recording patterns differ between species.

Each ridge shows that species' share of monthly observations.

Hover a ridge for monthly values. Peaks can reflect both animals and observer effort.

Fishing and protected wildlife cross paths.

AFMA logbooks record physical interactions between protected wildlife and Commonwealth fishing operations, allowing broad marine groups to be compared in one reporting system.

Reported interactions span several protected marine groups.

Counts aggregate species listed in AFMA's final Q1 2025 protected-species interaction report.

For January to March 2025, AFMA reported interactions involving protected sharks, seals and sea lions, marine turtles, and whales and dolphins.

An interaction means physical contact with people, vessels or fishing gear. These counts are not population estimates.

These categories are broader than the five focal species used elsewhere on the page.

The totals compare reported encounters, not total harm across Australian waters.

The quarter recorded 36 protected-shark, 35 seal or sea-lion, 20 marine-turtle and 12 whale-and-dolphin interactions.

Most turtle interactions were recorded alive, while whales and dolphins had a larger recorded dead share.

Together, the charts separate interaction counts from recorded life status.

Life status differs across the reported groups.

Bars show the share recorded alive, dead or injured within each broad wildlife group.

Marine park networks use different management rules.

The treemap compares how each park network divides its area among management types, making different protection approaches easier to compare.

The Roman numerals are official IUCN management codes, not scores. The guide below gives the plain-English management type for each code.

Code IaStrict nature reserveMinimal human disturbance.
Code IINational parkEcosystem protection; extraction tightly limited.
Code IVHabitat / species managementTargeted management protects habitats or species.
Code VISustainable multiple useConservation with sustainable resource use.

Management categories describe objectives, not a ranking of park quality.

Sources behind the story

All chart sources are collected here rather than repeated underneath individual visualisations.

Primary datasets

  • Atlas of Living Australia - focal-species occurrence records. Each download has its own DOI, listed below.
  • DCCEEW Biologically Important Areas - migration, reproduction, foraging and resting polygons.
  • DCCEEW Australian Marine Parks - park networks, zoning and area.
  • ABS state and territory boundaries (ASGS Edition 4) - geographic context for the maps.
  • Australian Fisheries Management Authority protected-species interaction reports - Commonwealth fishery logbook interactions with protected wildlife, including reported life status. The human-interaction charts use the final report for 1 January to 31 March 2025.
